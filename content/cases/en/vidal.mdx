<Chapter id="probleme" title="The problem">

Vidal has the most reliable drug information there is, but it is written for healthcare professionals.

Its business relies on doctors, pharmacists and software vendors, who pay to make prescriptions safer. The general public had never really been served. Yet 70,000 individuals had already created an account on vidal.fr.

A research phase Vidal ran with patients, caregivers and clinicians had confirmed the need. The bet: turn a reference database into an app that responds to each patient's situation.

The design challenge had two sides:

- make highly technical medical information understandable, without distorting it;
- personalize alerts (interactions, contraindications) based on the patient's profile and treatments.

The app was also aimed at the upcoming app store of Mon espace santé, France's national digital health space, which came with security and data-exchange requirements.

<Question>How do you make reference medical information understandable to every patient, without distorting it?</Question>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="role" title="My role">

I designed the app's flows and screens, from V0 to V1.5.

The project ran at a tight pace: a daily check-in with the Vidal team and a weekly meeting with their working group. For V1.5, design workshops with Vidal's teams informed my choices.

The scope covered:

- scanning a drug box, and the drug page;
- tracking current treatments, and history;
- self-medication guidance and interaction alerts;
- the health profile, prescription storage and editorial content.

<Chapter id="d1" decision="Decision 01" title="A drug page that answers “can I take it?”">

I split the drug page into two levels: a summary, then a “learn more” section.

The summary answers a single question, broken down into three checks:

- is it compatible with me?
- is it still good, not expired?
- does it match my symptoms?

Vidal's full information is still there, but it no longer stands in the way of the answer. The tone draws on FALC, the French “easy to read and understand” standard.

<Chapter id="d2" decision="Decision 02" title="Graded alerts, not all at the same level">

Not all interactions are equal. A food interaction, for example, calls for caution, not alarm: it shows in orange, not red. The specs also define which foods to show, so the patient isn't buried in warnings.

<Chapter id="d3" decision="Decision 03" title="A health profile patients can actually fill in">

Vidal's engine personalizes alerts based on the profile: allergies, sex, kidney function. But it expects a creatinine clearance in ml/min, while labs report a different value. Asking patients for that number would have been tedious and error-prone.

The direction we chose: replace the lab value with a level of kidney failure (mild, moderate, severe), which the engine accepts instead.
