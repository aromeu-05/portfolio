<Chapter id="probleme" title="The problem">

For about fifteen years, Opportunity had sold messaging software that was too old to move to SaaS and had an outdated interface.

Its clients are large enterprises. They use it heavily for debt collection: chasing unpaid invoices by email, text or voice message, at scale. Launching the new SaaS platform by summer 2026 was a strategic priority for the company.

The heart of the product is the scenario: a sequence of messages that adapts to each contact. If the customer doesn't open the email within 48 hours, they get a text; if they click, the sequence stops. With nine channels, sending-hour rules by country and sending limits, the logic quickly becomes unreadable.

<Question>How do you make complex automation logic readable and editable for a marketer, without asking them to think like a developer?</Question>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="d1" decision="Decision 01" title="A scenario that reads like a diagram">

The editor is a visual canvas: you start from “Start of scenario,” which shows how many contacts are included, and add blocks connected to each other.

The palette on the left splits blocks into two families:

- **logic**: condition, A/B test, wait, end of scenario;
- **channels**: email, SMS, RCS, WhatsApp, voice note, callbot, each in a simple or conversational version.

The scenario can split by country right from the start, with each branch keeping its own sending-hour rules.

<Figure src="/images/opportunity/web/opportunity-01-ouverture-editeur-scenario.jpg" width={1440} height={840} caption="The scenario editor, with the Email block's settings panel open" />

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="d2" decision="Decision 02" title="Every message leads to reactions, not conditions to write">

Each sending block comes with four ready-made outputs: no interaction, partial interaction, goal reached, and error. The marketer doesn't write any rules: they click the “+” on an output to say what happens next.

The “Error” output is handled by the platform, which stops the scenario. A tooltip explains this, so nobody tries to connect it.

<Figure src="/images/opportunity/web/opportunity-02-zoom-quatre-sorties-bloc-email.jpg" width={340} height={355} maxWidth={340} caption="The four outputs of a sending block; the Error output is handled by the platform" />

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="d3" decision="Decision 03" title="Setting up a block in guided steps">

Clicking a block opens a numbered side panel: content, sender, analysis window, send throttling. Every technical setting comes with a plain-language explanation:

- the analysis window defaults to 48 hours, with a benchmark: after 96 hours, there is on average no more interaction;
- the hourly sending limit explains that it protects the sender's reputation.

The defaults do the work; experts can adjust them.

<Figure src="/images/opportunity/web/opportunity-01-ouverture-editeur-scenario.jpg" width={1440} height={840} caption="The step-by-step panel: content, sender, analysis window, throttling" />

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="d4" decision="Decision 04" title="Creating a scenario in five steps">

The first version put everything in the editor. I restructured it into a step-by-step flow, approved in May 2026:

1. the scenario's framework;
2. the recipients: a list, then a segment of that list, with the option to create one without leaving the flow;
3. the visual editor;
4. the settings: sending hours by country and webhooks;
5. a summary of the credits used, before launch.

The summary follows a rule agreed with Opportunity: each subscription caps credits per channel. The marketer sees what their scenario will cost before sending it.

The platform has been in development since April 2026, and Opportunity's business teams ran acceptance testing on it in June and July 2026.

These test sessions with real users surfaced very concrete feedback, such as aligning the WhatsApp terminology with Meta's, or a regulatory change to sending hours. They fed directly into the next round of design.

<Learn label="What I took away">In an automation tool, design doesn't simplify the logic, it makes it visible. Good defaults and explanations in the right place do the rest.</Learn>
